# Review examples

These before-and-after pairs show common antipatterns and how to fix them.

## X-not-Y pseudo-aphoristic

This common pattern uses contrast for punch when a direct instruction would be
clearer.

Before:
> It should provoke feedback, not look finished.

After:
> Give stakeholders a concrete draft they can review and improve.

Before:
> Written for the team, not for circulation.

After:
> This brief gives the team the context needed to continue the work. Keep it
> internal.

Before:
> That makes it a scoping document rather than a pitch. The section that does
> the most work is the one saying what we will not do.

After:
> The document should define the work to take on and set clear limits on the
> rest.

Before:
> Top three, not ten.

After:
> Limit the page to three commitments. Shorten both the current page and the
> menu accordingly.

Before:
> Technical leadership, not convening.

After:
> Focus the work on technical leadership. Use community events only when they
> support that work.

Before:
> Modest and bounded beats big every time.

After:
> Prefer modest, bounded claims. Avoid unsupported promises.

Before:
> Offer, don't demand. Grant permission. Lower the anxiety.

After:
> Give readers room to decide, reassure them about common difficulties and
> respect their time.

## Mic-drop

A short declarative that closes a section by asserting a universal truth. The
rhythm does the persuading and the claim is usually unsupported.

Before:
> The second is the one that teaches you.

After:
> For the engineering work I want to do, I learn more through delivery.

Before:
> By delivery: in the team room, in the code, on the hook for the outcome.

After:
> Delivery means joining the team, writing the code and taking responsibility
> for the outcome.

Before:
> Warm != salesy. The whole point.

After:
> Warmth should come from true, specific details.

Before:
> That is where the real learning happens.

After:
> I have learned more from shipping than from advising.

## Drumbeat cadence

Drumbeat cadence stacks verbless fragments to create an artificial rhythm.
Each item sounds punchy alone but the stack reads like a trailer.

Before:
> - **Strategy** - a standing seat in the leadership group.
> - **External presence** - public profile as an affiliated signal.
> - **Convening** - practitioners, across industry.
> - **Field intelligence** - recon into other organisations.
> - **Engagement** - a live relationship as a delivered seat.

After:
> Support the leadership group and represent the practice externally. Bring
> practitioners together, learn from other organisations and advise selected
> partners.

Before:
> - A reading list. Low cost, high visibility, a community artefact.
> - A news roundup as an overnight agent job. Tooling, not role content.

After:
> The idea bank also includes a reading list and an overnight agent that
> gathers relevant news. Both are low-effort items, not role commitments.

## Count-before-list

Before:
> Three deliverables, in priority order:

After:
> Produce the following deliverables in priority order.

## Cute label as pseudo-insight

Before:
> **The risk that matters most.** Those two needs together are a full-time role.

After:
> The main risk is that these two needs together would fill a full-time role.

[More examples](../docs/examples/cute-labels.md)

## Numbered unordered lists

Before:
> 1. **Superlatives and hype words** - most can be cut.
> 2. **Forward-looking promises** - replace with what is true now.
> 3. **Unearned enthusiasm** - downgrade to a plain full stop.

After:
> - **Superlatives and hype words** - most can be cut.
> - **Forward-looking promises** - replace with what is true now.
> - **Unearned enthusiasm** - downgrade to a plain full stop.
